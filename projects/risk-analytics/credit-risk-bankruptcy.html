Credit Risk Analytics

PG&E, before the fall

Three independent credit models, all flagging default risk above 50% before it happened

Pacific Gas and Electric filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in January 2019, driven by an estimated $30 billion in wildfire liability. This project builds a forward-looking credit assessment using the data available before that filing, triangulating three independent frameworks rather than relying on any single score, to test whether the deterioration was visible in advance.

02 / Why Triangulate Three Models

No single credit score is enough

The Altman Z-Score is a linear combination of five accounting ratios, working capital, retained earnings, EBIT, market value of equity, and sales, each scaled by total assets or liabilities. It is fast to compute and well understood, but it was built on manufacturing companies in the 1960s and can misfire on capital structures that look different from that base case, which is exactly the concern with a regulated utility like PG&E.

The Ohlson O-Score addresses that gap with a logistic regression trained on a broader, more contemporary sample, converting the same kind of accounting inputs into an explicit default probability rather than a score that needs a separate interpretation table. The Merton model takes a completely different approach, treating a firm's equity as a call option on its assets and backing out a distance-to-default from the volatility of that equity, which means it can react to market-implied risk faster than either accounting-based model, whose inputs only update when financial statements are filed.

Where credit risk analysis actually earns its keep is when these three independent lenses agree. If an accounting-based score, a probability-based score, and a market-implied structural model are all deteriorating together, that convergence is a much stronger signal than any one of them alone, and it is what this analysis found in PG&E's case.

03 / Benchmarking Against Rating Agency Data

Checked against real default studies

A model score only means something in context. This assessment does not stop at computing the three scores in isolation, it benchmarks PG&E's implied default probability against Fitch's 2015 and 2017 corporate default studies and Moody's 2017 default report, the same reference data rating agencies themselves use to calibrate what a given Z-Score or O-Score band actually implies about realised default rates. That context is what turns a raw model output into a defensible credit view rather than a number with no anchor.

Why this matters for the roleA credit risk analyst is not just running a formula. They are deciding whether a score of 1.8 versus 2.1 on the Altman scale is actually meaningful for this specific borrower, in this specific sector, against what similarly scored companies have historically done. Benchmarking against Fitch and Moody's default studies is what makes that judgement defensible rather than a guess.
04 / What This Demonstrates

Prediction, not just explanation

It is easy to explain a bankruptcy after it has already happened. The discipline in this project is building the assessment strictly from information that would have been available before PG&E's actual filing, then checking the output against what really occurred. All three independent frameworks converging on a bankruptcy probability above 50% ahead of the event is the kind of result that would actually change a credit committee's decision, not just narrate one that was already made.
